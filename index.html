Why it matters

Climate science speaks almost only in color.

Heat waves, pollution and storms reach the public as colored maps. For people who cannot see them, or cannot tell their colors apart, the data simply is not there, even though they are among the people most exposed to climate disasters.

Maps are closed doors

A colored map has no text a screen reader can read. A blind student or scientist has to wait for someone to describe it.

Rainbow scales mislead

Rainbow color maps distort data and are unreadable for many people with color-vision deficiency, about 1 in 12 men (Crameri et al., Nature Communications, 2020).

Those most at risk are left out

84% of people with disabilities have no personal disaster plan, and 86% feel excluded from local planning (UNDRR global survey, 2023).

One planet, four instruments

Every variable has its own voice.

Four global NASA GEOS maps, 372 hourly frames each (30 Oct – 14 Nov 2026). The higher the value, the higher the note, always on a pentatonic scale, so it always sounds good.

How it works

From NASA's pixels to your ears and hands, in four steps.

  1. 01

    Read NASA's map

    Each pixel is matched to the map's official color bar in the CIELAB color space and turned back into a physical value.

  2. 02

    Turn it into music

    The value picks the note; the cursor's position picks left or right ear. Ocean waves, polar wind and forest birds say where you are.

  3. 03

    Ask, and a voice answers

    Press Space to hear the country and the exact value. The music pauses while the voice speaks, so sounds never compete.

  4. 04

    Feel it on your phone

    Scan a QR code and your phone vibrates with the data over WebRTC: slow, weak pulses for low values; fast, strong ones for high.

Designed for keyboards and screen readers from day one.

Every action is one key, with no Ctrl, Alt or Shift. A five-minute audio tutorial teaches it all with hands-on challenges on a real map.

Low vision and color blindness: large text, high contrast, Cividis and Viridis palettes and a color-blindness simulator.

Questions

Do I need to install anything?

No. It runs in any modern browser. Headphones help you hear left and right.

Is the data real?

Yes. Every value comes from maps published by NASA's Global Modeling and Assimilation Office (GEOS model), decoded through their official color bars.

Does it work with my screen reader?

It is built with ARIA for NVDA, JAWS, VoiceOver and TalkBack. If you use one, turn off the site's own voice in the Accessibility panel.

Does the vibration work on iPhone?

iPhone browsers block web vibration, so the phone shows visual pulses instead. On Android it vibrates.

Ready to hear the planet?

Put on headphones, press Enter, and let the audio tutorial guide you.
